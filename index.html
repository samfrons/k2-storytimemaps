Chapter I

The Socialite

Dudley Wolfe had crossed an ocean under sail and driven an ambulance through a world war. The one thing he never mastered was climbing.

Dudley Francis Cecil Wolfe was born on February 6, 1896, into a world of inherited wealth and invented pedigree. His father, an English immigrant coffee importer, mixed in New York society on entirely fictitious stories of an aristocratic past. His mother Mabel was the daughter of Benjamin Franklin Smith, whose Colorado mining fortune — worth $70 to $100 million by 1927 — made him reputedly the richest man in New England, roughly a billion dollars and more in today's money.

When the U.S. military rejected him in 1916 — poor eyesight, flat feet — Wolfe joined the Red Cross ambulance corps and drove a converted Model T through the killing fields of the Great War, later earning the Croce di Guerra with the Italian Ambulance Service. Called up at last by the French Foreign Legion in October 1918, he served one month before the Armistice. His old school, which had once expelled him for poor grades, now acclaimed him as its most decorated former student.

In 1929 he commissioned a sixty-foot schooner, Mohawk, and became the first person to race a sixty-foot yacht across the Atlantic — finishing second in the King and Queen's Cup against vessels of a hundred feet and more, captaining her himself.

He entered Harvard at 29 despite lacking the qualifications and graduated in 1930. He married skiing pioneer Alice Damrosch in 1934, asked for a divorce in 1938 — and they went on living together anyway. He skied across the Mont Blanc massif. Rock climbing, with all its technicalities, he tried and never mastered; in the spring of 1939 he failed to summit Mont Blanc and Piz Palü even with a guide.

In early 1938, in their Fifth Avenue apartment, Wolfe and Alice projected slides of their Alpine seasons for their friends. Among the guests was Fritz Wiessner — quietly scanning the room for wealthy mountaineers who might fund his obsession.

Chapter II

Chosen for Wealth,
Not for Mountains

Every qualified climber Wiessner invited said no. What sailed from Genoa was a team selected by bank balance.

With the American economy in poor shape, there was no public or private funding to be had for 1939. Wiessner had to build a team from men who could pay their own way — $2,500 each, six months from home. Every strong climber he invited turned him down: Bill House, Adams Carter, Sterling Hendricks, Roger Whitney, Alfred Lindley. House later admitted his refusal was down to personal differences with Wiessner.

What assembled instead was a team that Jack Durrance, in his private diary, judged under-qualified — every man of them, himself included, save Wiessner.

German-born, naturalized 1935. Nanga Parbat 1932. First ascent of Mount Waddington. The pre-eminent American climber — and the only one on the team with experience on an 8,000-meter peak. Brilliant, forceful, obsessed with K2 since he first saw it from 130 miles away.

Climber · Age 43
Substantially bankrolling the expedition. Bulky and clumsy on rock, but strong, tireless under hardship, and well-liked. Would climb higher than any American except Wiessner — and never come back down.
TC
Tony Cromwell
Deputy Leader · Age 46
Independently wealthy, with more ascents than anyone in the AAC — all of them behind guides. Said from the start he would not climb above Camp IV. His July 18 order to clear the lower camps would become the center of the storm.
JD
Jack Durrance
Expedition Doctor · Age 26
A last-minute replacement, appointed after the others had sailed. Bavarian boyhood, Teton guide — and no actual medical training yet. His high-altitude boots arrived three weeks late. He would keep his diary sealed for fifty years.
CC
Chappell Cranmer
Climber · Age 20
Dartmouth student, Wiessner's climbing partner in 1938. Collapsed at Base Camp — probably pulmonary edema — and was saved by two hours of Durrance's artificial respiration. He might have been Wiessner's best climber. His expedition was over on day two.
GS
George Sheldon
Climber · Age 20
Cranmer's Dartmouth classmate, with a little Tetons experience and great enthusiasm. Severely frostbitten toes ended his climb; he left for home before the disaster began.

And there were the nine Sherpas from Darjeeling, led by sirdar Pasang Kikuli — Nanda Devi 1936, K2 1938: the most experienced high-altitude climber in the world. His deputy was Pasang Dawa Lama. Four of the nine had been on K2 the year before. They would prove more capable than most of the men they served — and they shared no common language with them.

Chapter III

The Savage Mountain

28,251 feet. Three expeditions had already failed on it. The 1938 party had left a route open like a door ajar.

The Karakoram

K2 stands 28,251 feet above sea level on what was then the border of British India and China — the second-highest mountain on Earth, the highest point of the Karakoram, and the first great peak to catch every storm rolling in across Central Asia.

Just reaching it took a month: 330 miles from the roadhead at Wayil, by foot and pony, over the Zoji La and up the Baltoro Glacier. Cromwell called it a bleak and barren country. On May 31 the party turned into the Godwin-Austen Glacier and saw K2 at last.

The Abruzzi Ridge

In 1909 the Duke of the Abruzzi's expedition — with Vittorio Sella hauling 30×40 cm photographic plates across the glaciers — reached about 20,510 feet on the southeast ridge before declaring the mountain unclimbable. The ridge took the Duke's name anyway.

In 1938, Charlie Houston's American reconnaissance climbed that same ridge to about 26,000 feet, mapped the camps, solved the House Chimney, and came home with a clear route to the summit.

The way was open. Wiessner needed only to follow it.

Vittorio Sella, 1859–1943 · The photographs on this page of K2 before the age of color were made by Sella and the Duke of the Abruzzi’s 1909 expedition — thirty years before Wiessner’s party pitched its tents on the ridge that took the Duke’s name.

Chapter IV · June 1 — July 31, 1939

Fifty-three days on the Abruzzi Spur

What follows is the expedition day by day, over the real terrain of K2 — every dated position drawn from the expedition record. Each dot is a person. Scroll, and watch the gap widen — fatally — between the summit party and the world below.

Camp locations are placed approximately along the Abruzzi route; altitudes are as documented in 1939.

June 1, 1939

Base Camp Established

After a month-long trek from Srinagar — 330 miles by foot and pony — the expedition establishes Base Camp at 16,500 feet on the Godwin-Austen Glacier.

The next day, Cranmer collapses with pulmonary edema. Durrance saves his life with two hours of artificial respiration. Wiessner's most promising young climber is finished.

Porters are sent back to Askole with instructions to return on July 23. They have 53 days to climb the mountain.

June 14, 1939

Wiessner Takes the Lead

Camp II established as the main supply depot — 3,360 pounds of stores. Wiessner discovers Cromwell has barely advanced beyond the 1938 sites. He takes over all lead climbing.

Wolfe is slow but tireless and well-liked. He associates more closely with Wiessner — possibly because he is paying most of the bills. The team is splitting along class lines.

June 21–29, 1939

The Eight-Day Storm

A storm pins the expedition for eight days. At Camp IV the temperature drops to −2°F (−19°C). At Camp II, hurricane-force gusts reach 80 mph.

When the weather clears, Wiessner and Wolfe still want to summit. Everyone else has lost the will.

Durrance's boots finally arrive on June 28. Sheldon has severe frostbite and is sent down permanently.

June 30 – July 1, 1939

The House Chimney

Wiessner scales the House Chimney — the crux gully Bill House had led over four hours in 1938 — fixes ropes, and pulls Pasang Kikuli up after him.

The next day, on a very tight rope, he hauls Wolfe and another Sherpa up the cliffs. The four establish Camp V at 22,000 feet, right above the chimney, where the sharp part of the ridge begins.

July 6, 1939

Camp VII — Top of the Ridge

Wiessner scales the House Chimney, fixes ropes, and pushes through the Black Pyramid to establish Camp VII at 24,700 feet — the top of the Abruzzi Ridge.

No supplies have reached even Camp IV. Wiessner descends all the way to Camp II to find out why. The support team is astonished by his progress but has barely been functioning.

July 13, 1939

Durrance Collapses

Ascending to Camp VII, Durrance collapses with altitude sickness — likely cerebral or pulmonary edema. He barely manages to descend to Camp VI.

He leaves rushed instructions for the Sherpas to ferry supplies upward — but they share no common language with the Americans. The instructions may not have been understood.

The supply chain is now broken. Above, three climbers and three Sherpas continue toward the summit. Below, no one is fit to climb.

July 14, 1939

Camp VIII — The Shoulder

Wiessner, Wolfe, and Pasang Dawa Lama reach Camp VIII at 25,300 feet on The Shoulder.

Durrance has retreated all the way to Camp II where he finds Cromwell and Trench in complete apathy.

Dudley Wolfe has now been above 22,000 feet for 24 continuous days. His body is slowly deteriorating in the thin air — though the full effects of prolonged extreme altitude were not yet understood.

July 17, 1939

Wolfe Turns Back at the Bergschrund

The summit assault begins. At the bergschrund above Camp VIII it becomes clear Wolfe can climb no higher. He returns to Camp VIII to wait. He will never leave it under his own power.

Wiessner and Pasang Lama push on, establishing Camp IX at 26,050 feet on a rock pillar. Provisions for 6 days. Fuel for longer.

July 19, 1939

800 Feet from the Summit

9:00 AM. Perfect weather. Wiessner chooses the harder rock route over the easier Bottleneck couloir.

Nine hours of unprecedented technical climbing. By nightfall they stand at ~27,450 feet. Only 800 feet of easy snow plateau separates them from the top.

Wiessner wants to push through the moonlit night. Pasang Lama refuses and will not pay out the rope. Wiessner agrees to descend.

Below, Sherpa Tse Tendrup has reached 500 feet below Camp VIII. He shouts three times. Gets no reply. Concludes everyone above is dead.

July 20–21, 1939

The Camps Are Stripped

Believing the summit party dead, Sherpas in the middle camps strip Camps VII and VI of tents and sleeping bags as they descend.

July 21: Wiessner and Pasang Lama try the Bottleneck route, but without crampons the snow is impossible. The summit is finished.

Sheldon and Cranmer leave for home — Dartmouth term begins soon. They will miss the catastrophe entirely.

July 22, 1939

Wolfe Found Alone

Wiessner and Pasang Lama descend to Camp VIII expecting resupply. They find Wolfe alone, out of matches, unable to cook or melt ice. He has been alone for over a week.

Descending to Camp VII, Wolfe's clumsiness causes a fall on the rope that nearly kills all three. His sleeping bag is lost.

At Camp VII: tents collapsed, no mattresses, one sleeping bag, food scattered in the snow. Wiessner leaves Wolfe here to descend further for help.

Dudley Wolfe is now alone at 24,700 feet with nothing.

July 24, 1939

The Recriminations

Wiessner and Pasang Lama stagger into Base Camp, barely able to walk. Pasang Lama is in dire condition.

Wiessner is furious. He accuses Cromwell of attempting to murder them. Cromwell accuses Wiessner of abandoning Wolfe. They become enemies for life.

Durrance keeps quiet about his role in clearing the lower camps — it had been under Cromwell's orders.

July 28, 1939

Kikuli’s Impossible Climb

Pasang Kikuli and Tsering Norbu leave Base Camp at 6:00 AM. By noon they reach Camp IV. By evening — Camp VI.

They have climbed 7,000 vertical feet in a single day — almost 1,000 feet per hour. Western climbers would not match this alpine-style ascent for decades.

Cromwell and Trench have already left, leading the porters back to Askole.

July 29, 1939

The Sherpas Reach Wolfe

Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar, and Phinsoo reach Wolfe at Camp VII. What they find is devastating.

He has been alone at over 24,000 feet for a week. Trapped in his tent, covered in urine and feces, utterly apathetic. He has spent 38 continuous days above 22,000 feet — unmatched at the time, even with oxygen.

He is uninterested in the letters they bring. He refuses to descend.

He tells them to come back tomorrow — he will be ready then.

July 31, 1939

The Last Ascent

After being stormbound on July 30, the three Sherpas climb to Camp VII for a third time.

Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar, Phinsoo,
and Dudley Wolfe were never seen alive again.

Tsering Norbu waits two days at Camp VI, then runs down. Durrance writes: "The Sherpas are certain something awful has happened."

In 2002 — sixty-three years later — Wolfe's remains were found on the Godwin-Austen Glacier with fragments of his tent and a leather mitten marked "Wolfe."

August 2, 1939

Something Awful

Tsering Norbu waits two days at Camp VI, then runs down the mountain in a single morning.

Durrance writes in his diary: "The Sherpas are certain something awful has happened."

Wiessner refuses to believe it — it seems impossible to him that anything could have happened to so able a group.

August 3–7, 1939

The Last Search

With no one left fit to climb, Wiessner sets out anyway with Tsering Norbu and Dawa Thondup. It takes them all day to reach Camp I.

A storm pins them there until August 7. Then Tsering Norbu corrects his earlier account: they had found Wolfe at Camp VII with no food at all.

Even Wiessner loses hope. Wolfe and the three Sherpas are gone. The glacier keeps them for sixty-three years.

Chapter V · July 19, 1939

800 Feet from Glory

No one had ever stood on an 8,000-meter summit. By nightfall, two men were an easy snow plateau away from being first.

09:00 · Camp IX · 26,050 ft

Wiessner and Pasang Dawa Lama left camp in perfect weather — a late start, with the summit 2,200 feet above them.

At a point of decision, Wiessner faced two ways up: a traverse right into the couloir later named the Bottleneck, beneath unstable ice from the summit cornice — or a direct rock climb to the left, free of falling ice but brutally difficult. He chose the rock.

Nightfall · ~27,450 ft

Nine hours of climbing followed, at a technical standard unprecedented at that altitude — a line that, as of Viesturs and Roberts' 2009 account, has never been repeated.

By dark they stood at roughly 27,450 feet. Above them lay 800 feet of easy snow. Wiessner wanted to push on through the night and finish it.

Pasang Lama refused, and would not pay out the rope. Wiessner agreed to turn back — he did not go on alone, and through all the bitterness to come, he never once criticized his partner.

The Descent

Going back down the cliffs in darkness, Pasang Lama lost both pairs of crampons he was carrying. They reached camp at 2:30 in the morning.

"I regretted many times on the way down that I had given in." — Fritz Wiessner
July 21 · The Second Attempt

They tried again by the Bottleneck. Without crampons, in bad snow, it was hopeless. They returned to Camp IX. The summit was finished.

Jim Curran's later verdict: had Wiessner taken the couloir on the 19th, the pair might have summited and returned to camp the same day — the first humans atop an eight-thousander, without oxygen, fifteen years before Annapurna — and, with the high camps still stocked and manned as Wiessner believed they were, everyone might have come home.

Chapter VI

The Long Wait

While two men fought for the summit, the mountain beneath them was quietly emptying out. His dot still marks Camp VII.

While Wiessner climbed, Dudley Wolfe waited at Camp VIII, where his body had given out at the bergschrund on July 17. By the time Wiessner and Pasang Lama came back down on July 22, Wolfe had been alone for days — out of matches, unable to cook or even melt ice for water. Descending together toward Camp VII, Wolfe stumbled on the rope and nearly pulled all three men off the mountain; his sleeping bag was lost in the fall.

At Camp VII, a second shock: the tents collapsed under snow, no mattresses, one sleeping bag among three men, food scattered. The camp had been stripped.

What had happened below was a cascade of misread signals. On July 20 the Sherpa Tse Tendrup had climbed to within 500 feet of Camp VIII and shouted, three times, into the silence. No answer came. Seeing fresh avalanche debris, he concluded the summit party was dead — and the Sherpas, seeing the lower camps already being cleared on Cromwell's July 18 order, struck Camps VI and VII on the way down. By July 23 they were at Base Camp.

Wiessner left Wolfe at Camp VII — at Wolfe's own request, he later said — and descended with Pasang Lama, expecting to find supplies one camp down, still intending another summit attempt. Camp by camp they found nothing: little food, no sleeping bags, no people. They staggered into Base Camp on July 24, barely able to walk. Wiessner, enraged, accused first Cromwell and then Tendrup of trying to murder him. Cromwell, in turn, accused Wiessner of abandoning Wolfe. They were enemies for life within the hour.

Wolfe was now the highest human being on Earth — and entirely alone.

The rescue · From the expedition record

July 25 — Durrance sets out with Dawa Thondup, Phinsoo and Pasang Kitar; exhausted, he and Dawa Thondup turn back from Camp IV while the two Sherpas continue to Camp VI.

July 28 — Pasang Kikuli and Tsering Norbu leave Base Camp at 06:00 and reach Camp VI by nightfall: 7,000 vertical feet in a single day, an alpine-style ascent Western climbers would not match for decades.

July 29, noon — Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar and Phinsoo reach Wolfe at Camp VII. He has now spent 38 continuous days above 22,000 feet — at the time, no one had done this even with bottled oxygen. They find him apathetic, trapped in a fouled tent, uninterested in his letters. He refuses to descend, telling them to come back the next day, when he will be ready.

July 31 — After a day stormbound at Camp VI, the same three Sherpas climb toward Camp VII a second time.

Chapter VII

Fifty Years of Silence

The blame shifted like snow — from Wiessner, to Durrance, and at last, half a century on, to where the diary said it belonged.

On August 2, Tsering Norbu ran down from Camp VI to Base Camp in a morning. Durrance wrote in his diary: "The Sherpas are certain something awful has happened." Wiessner refused to believe it — it seemed impossible to him that anything could have happened to so able a group. His own last search, on August 3, collapsed under a four-day storm at Camp I. By August 7, even Wiessner had lost hope. Four men were gone.

The recriminations began at Bandipora on August 27, when Cromwell saw Wiessner's draft report and — by Durrance's diary, the only record of the scene — shouted that Wiessner had murdered Wolfe and the Sherpas. Cromwell and Trench had already cabled the AAC and spread their account through the British community at Srinagar. Back in America, Wiessner gave a disastrous interview to the New York Times, saying that in the high mountains, as in war, one had to expect casualties. The climbing world took sides. An AAC inquiry produced a report so bland it settled nothing. Both Wiessner and Cromwell resigned from the club.

For seventeen years, Wiessner bore the public blame. Then in 1956 he published his account and produced the story of a crumpled note — found, he said, on the floor of Camp II, in Durrance's handwriting, announcing the order to bring down the sleeping bags. Durrance never publicly refuted it. The blame swung to him, and stayed there. Wiessner was rehabilitated, made an honorary member of the AAC in 1966, and by the 1980s was revered.

Durrance said nothing. For thirty-three more years.

Wiessner died in 1988. Only then, in 1989, did Durrance open his handwritten expedition diary to the researchers Kauffman and Putnam. It recorded that the order to clear the lower camps had come from Cromwell, on July 18 — and that if a note had gone up the mountain, it was most likely Cromwell's note to the Sherpas at Camp VII. Durrance, they concluded, had kept silent for fifty years to protect two men who were now both dead. The crumpled note itself, which Wiessner said he had handed to the AAC inquiry, was never found, despite careful searches of the archives.

"One of the worst tragedies in the climbing history of the Himalaya." — Fosco Maraini, 1961

And yet, as Jim Curran wrote, it was so nearly an outstanding success. One route choice below the summit; one shout answered at Camp VIII; one order, better understood. In 1995, remains believed to be the Sherpas' were found on the Godwin-Austen Glacier. In 2002 the glacier gave up Dudley Wolfe: skeletal remains, vintage gear, and a leather mitten marked "Wolfe."

The Evidence

The Note That Was Never Found

Two accounts, published thirty-three years apart. Tap each document to weigh it.

Published 1956 · Seventeen years after

Wiessner’s account

Wiessner produced the story of a crumpled note, found on the floor of the tent at Camp II during his descent. It was, he said, in Durrance’s handwriting, and it announced the order to bring the sleeping bags down the mountain.

He said he had handed the note to the American Alpine Club’s committee of inquiry in 1939. Durrance never publicly refuted the story — and bore the blame for over thirty years.

EXHIBIT: the note itself — never found, despite careful searches of the archives.
Opened 1989 · After Wiessner’s death

Durrance’s diary

For fifty years Durrance said nothing. Only after Wiessner died in 1988 did he open his handwritten expedition diary to the researchers Kauffman and Putnam.

It records that the order to clear the lower camps came from Cromwell, on July 18 — and that a note carried up to the Sherpas at Camp VII was most likely Cromwell’s, not his.

EXHIBIT: the diary — kept sealed for half a century.

In Memoriam

Those who did not return

DW
Dudley Francis Cecil Wolfe
February 6, 1896 — c. July 30, 1939 · Camp VII, 24,700 ft
Socialite, decorated ambulance driver, transatlantic racing captain, Harvard graduate, heir. Gentle, shy, stubborn. He spent 38 continuous days above 22,000 feet without oxygen — an endurance no one had then matched even with it. The glacier returned him in 2002, with a mitten bearing his name.
PK
Pasang Kikuli
Sirdar · Nanda Devi 1936 · K2 1938 & 1939
The most experienced high-altitude climber in the world. Climbed 7,000 feet in a single day to reach the man everyone else had given up on — then went up one final time on July 31.
PK
Pasang Kitar
Sherpa · Rescue Party
Went high on the first rescue attempt, reached Wolfe on the second, and volunteered again for the last. Last seen climbing toward Camp VII on July 31, 1939.
P
Phinsoo
Sherpa · K2 1938 · Rescue Party
Veteran of the 1938 reconnaissance. Reached Wolfe at Camp VII on July 29 and climbed toward him again two days later. Disappeared with Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar, and Wolfe.
