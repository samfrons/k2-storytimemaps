Chapter I

The Socialite

Dudley Wolfe had crossed an ocean under sail and driven an ambulance through a world war. The one thing he never mastered was climbing.

Dudley Francis Cecil Wolfe was born on February 6, 1896, into a world of inherited wealth and invented pedigree. His father, an English immigrant coffee importer, mixed in New York society on entirely fictitious stories of an aristocratic past. His mother Mabel was the daughter of Benjamin Franklin Smith, whose Colorado mining fortune — worth $70 to $100 million by 1927 — made him reputedly the richest man in New England, roughly a billion dollars and more in today's money.

When the U.S. military rejected him in 1916 — poor eyesight, flat feet — Wolfe joined the Red Cross ambulance corps and drove a converted Model T through the killing fields of the Great War, later earning the Croce di Guerra with the Italian Ambulance Service. Called up at last by the French Foreign Legion in October 1918, he served one month before the Armistice. His old school, which had once expelled him for poor grades, now acclaimed him as its most decorated former student.

In 1929 he commissioned a sixty-foot schooner, Mohawk, and became the first person to race a sixty-foot yacht across the Atlantic — finishing second in the King and Queen's Cup against vessels of a hundred feet and more, captaining her himself.

He entered Harvard at 29 despite lacking the qualifications and graduated in 1930. He married skiing pioneer Alice Damrosch in 1934, asked for a divorce in 1938 — and they went on living together anyway. He skied across the Mont Blanc massif. Rock climbing, with all its technicalities, he tried and never mastered; in the spring of 1939 he failed to summit Mont Blanc and Piz Palü even with a guide.

In early 1938, in their Fifth Avenue apartment, Wolfe and Alice projected slides of their Alpine seasons for their friends. Among the guests was Fritz Wiessner — quietly scanning the room for wealthy mountaineers who might fund his obsession.

Chapter II

Chosen for Wealth,
Not for Mountains

Every qualified climber Wiessner invited said no. What sailed from Genoa was a team selected by bank balance.

With the American economy in poor shape, there was no public or private funding to be had for 1939. Wiessner had to build a team from men who could pay their own way — $2,500 each, about $17,500 all told, six months from home. Every strong climber he invited turned him down: Bill House, Adams Carter, Sterling Hendricks, Roger Whitney, Alfred Lindley. House later admitted his refusal was down to personal differences with Wiessner.

What assembled instead was a team that Jack Durrance, in his private diary, judged under-qualified — every man of them, himself included, save Wiessner.

Dartmouth student, Wiessner's climbing partner in 1938. Collapsed at Base Camp — probably pulmonary edema — and was saved by two hours of Durrance's artificial respiration. He might have been Wiessner's best climber. His expedition was over on day two.

Climber · Age 20
Cranmer's Dartmouth classmate, with a little Tetons experience and great enthusiasm. Severely frostbitten toes ended his climb; he left for home before the disaster began.

And there were the nine Sherpas from Darjeeling, led by sirdar Pasang Kikuli — Nanda Devi 1936, K2 1938: the most experienced high-altitude climber in the world. His deputy was Pasang Dawa Lama. Four of the nine had been on K2 the year before. They would prove more capable than most of the men they served — and they shared no common language with them.

1939 · Durrance Coll. · AAC
Pasang Kikuli
Sirdar · Age 28 · Sola Khumbu
On the great peaks since age 18: all three Kangchenjunga expeditions, Everest 1933, and Nanga Parbat 1934 — where he stayed beside the dying Uli Wieland at Camp VII, came down with dreadful frostbite, and was awarded the German Red Cross Medal. On Nanda Devi in 1936, Art Emmons called him "by far the best porter." Sirdar of K2 in 1938, and now again. A wife and two young children waited in Darjeeling.
PL
Pasang Dawa Lama
Deputy Sirdar · Age ~27
Born near Namche Bazaar and trained as a Nyingma Buddhist lama. First ascent of Chomolhari (7,326 m) with Freddy Spencer Chapman in 1937; Himalayan Club Tiger Badge, 1939. The strongest technical climber among the Sherpas — he would be Wiessner's rope-mate for the summit.
DT
Dawa Thondup
Sherpa · Age 32
Everest 1933; a survivor of the 1934 Nanga Parbat disaster, which took his younger brother. Saltoro Kangri 1935; Masherbrum 1938, carrying to 23,500 feet. Like Kikuli, he wore the German Red Cross Medal and a Tiger Badge.

On March 29, 1939, the team boarded the Italian liner Conte Biancamano at Genoa and sailed for Bombay, arriving April 10 — Europe sliding toward war behind them. At the pier came word that Vittorio Sella himself — the grand old man of mountain photography, veteran of the Duke of the Abruzzi's 1909 expedition, whose photographs appear throughout this page — wished to meet the men going back to his mountain. His advice traveled with them: it was Sella's recommendation to study the northeast ridge that sent Wiessner out on a reconnaissance on June 1, before he settled on the Abruzzi.

At Srinagar, Wolfe was dismayed to learn that his advice to bring two-way radios had not been taken. On the mountain, every message would move only as fast as a man could climb.

"We sat enraptured in our orchard campground as apricot blossom petals rained down upon us and floated in our teacups. This steeply terraced village of pink blossoms and green fields seemed a fairyland of spring only possible in a poet's imagination." — Chappell Cranmer, American Alpine Journal, 1940

The approach had harder days. At Urdukas the porters, unwilling to go on in bad weather, struck for higher pay — then changed their minds, the rates unchanged, with only three spare days of tsampa in reserve. There were not enough snow goggles to go around: makeshift glasses were cut from cardboard with narrow slits, and three porters went snow-blind anyway and were sent back to Askole, their loads divided among Cranmer, Durrance, Sheldon and Trench.

Chapter III

The Savage Mountain

28,251 feet. Three expeditions had already failed on it. The 1938 party had left a route open like a door ajar.

The Karakoram

K2 stands 28,251 feet above sea level on what was then the border of British India and China — the second-highest mountain on Earth, the highest point of the Karakoram, and the first great peak to catch every storm rolling in across Central Asia.

Just reaching it took a month: 330 miles from the roadhead at Wayil, by foot and pony, over the Zoji La and up the Baltoro Glacier. Cromwell called it a bleak and barren country. On May 31 the party turned into the Godwin-Austen Glacier and saw K2 at last.

The Abruzzi Ridge

In 1909 the Duke of the Abruzzi's expedition — with Vittorio Sella hauling 30×40 cm photographic plates across the glaciers — reached about 20,510 feet on the southeast ridge before declaring the mountain unclimbable. The ridge took the Duke's name anyway.

In 1938, Charlie Houston's American reconnaissance climbed that same ridge to about 26,000 feet, mapped the camps, solved the House Chimney, and came home with a clear route to the summit.

The way was open. Wiessner needed only to follow it.

Vittorio Sella, 1859–1943 · The photographs on this page of K2 before the age of color were made by Sella and the Duke of the Abruzzi’s 1909 expedition — thirty years before Wiessner’s party pitched its tents on the ridge that took the Duke’s name.

Chapter IV · June 1 — July 31, 1939

Fifty-three days on the Abruzzi Spur

What follows is the expedition day by day, over the real terrain of K2 — every dated position drawn from the expedition record. Each dot is a person. Scroll, and watch the gap widen — fatally — between the summit party and the world below.

Camp locations are placed approximately along the Abruzzi route; altitudes are as documented in 1939.

June 1, 1939

Base Camp Established

After a month-long trek from Srinagar — 330 miles by foot and pony — the expedition establishes Base Camp at 16,500 feet on the Godwin-Austen Glacier.

The next day, Cranmer collapses with pulmonary edema. Durrance saves his life with two hours of artificial respiration. He will not go high again.

Porters are sent back to Askole with instructions to return on July 23. They have 53 days to climb the mountain.

June 14, 1939

Wiessner Takes the Lead

Camp II established as the main supply depot — 3,360 pounds of stores. Wiessner discovers Cromwell has barely advanced beyond the 1938 sites. He takes over all lead climbing.

Wolfe, slow but tireless, climbs with him. The rest of the team settles into the lower camps.

June 21–29, 1939

The Eight-Day Storm

A storm pins the expedition for eight days. At Camp IV the temperature drops to −2°F (−19°C). At Camp II, hurricane-force gusts reach 80 mph.

Wiessner, in the expedition report: the gusts struck the tents "sounding like veritable machine-guns… To describe these days and nights of storm and cold is not within my power. They were terror-inspiring."

Durrance's boots finally arrive on June 28. Sheldon has severe frostbite and is sent down permanently.

June 30 – July 1, 1939

The House Chimney

Wiessner scales the House Chimney — the crux gully Bill House had led over four hours in 1938 — fixes ropes, and pulls Pasang Kikuli up after him. "I can only commend House for his ability in having originally led up this piece of difficult rock climbing," he would write in the expedition report.

The next day, on a very tight rope, he hauls Wolfe and another Sherpa up the cliffs. The four establish Camp V at 22,000 feet, right above the chimney, where the sharp part of the ridge begins.

July 6, 1939

Camp VII — Top of the Ridge

Above the chimney, Wiessner leads the party up the ice slopes and out through the rock towers of the Black Pyramid to establish Camp VII at 24,700 feet — the top of the Abruzzi Ridge.

No supplies have reached even Camp IV. Wiessner descends all the way to Camp II to find out why.

July 13, 1939

Durrance Collapses

Ascending to Camp VII, Durrance collapses with altitude sickness — likely cerebral or pulmonary edema. He barely manages to descend to Camp VI.

The supply chain is now broken. Above, three climbers and three Sherpas continue toward the summit. Below, no one is fit to climb.

July 14, 1939

Camp VIII — The Shoulder

Wiessner, Wolfe, and Pasang Dawa Lama reach Camp VIII at 25,300 feet on The Shoulder. Below them at Camp VII they leave a stocked depot — "two tents, two stoves, six quarts of fuel, three sleeping-bags, and twelve days' food."

Durrance has retreated all the way to Camp II.

July 17, 1939

Wolfe Turns Back at the Bergschrund

The summit assault begins. At the bergschrund above Camp VIII it becomes clear Wolfe can climb no higher. He returns to Camp VIII to wait.

Wiessner and Pasang Lama push on, establishing Camp IX at 26,050 feet on a rock pillar. Provisions for 6 days. Fuel for longer.

By this day Wiessner has spent 24 days above 22,000 feet, and Dudley Wolfe 26.

July 19, 1939

800 Feet from the Summit

9:00 AM, perfect weather: Wiessner and Pasang Dawa Lama leave Camp IX for the summit.

By nightfall they stand at ~27,450 feet — 800 feet below the top. In darkness they turn back, and reach camp at 2:30 in the morning.

July 20–21, 1939

The Camps Are Stripped

Sherpas descending from the middle camps strip Camps VI and VII — tents, mattresses, sleeping bags.

July 21: Wiessner and Pasang Lama make a second attempt and turn back low. They return to Camp IX.

Sheldon and Cranmer leave Base Camp for home — Dartmouth term begins soon.

July 22, 1939

Wolfe Found Alone

Wiessner and Pasang Lama descend to Camp VIII and find Wolfe alone — he has been there since July 17.

Roping down toward Camp VII, a fall nearly takes all three off the mountain. Wolfe's sleeping bag is lost.

At Camp VII, Wolfe stays. Wiessner and Pasang Lama continue down. Dudley Wolfe is now alone at 24,700 feet.

July 24–25, 1939

Back to Base Camp

Wiessner and Pasang Lama stagger into Base Camp, barely able to walk. Pasang Lama is in dire condition. Wolfe remains at Camp VII, alone.

July 25: Durrance starts back up with Dawa Thondup, Phinsoo, and Pasang Kitar. Exhausted, he and Dawa Thondup turn back from Camp IV; the two Sherpas continue to Camp VI.

July 28, 1939

Kikuli’s Impossible Climb

Pasang Kikuli and Tsering Norbu leave Base Camp at 6:00 AM. By noon they reach Camp IV. By evening — Camp VI.

They have climbed 7,000 vertical feet in a single day — almost 1,000 feet per hour. Western climbers would not match this alpine-style ascent for decades.

Cromwell and Trench have already left, leading the porters back to Askole.

July 29, 1939

The Sherpas Reach Wolfe

At noon, Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar, and Phinsoo reach Wolfe at Camp VII. He has been alone there for a week.

He refuses to descend. He tells them to come back tomorrow — he will be ready then.

The three return to Camp VI for the night.

July 31, 1939

The Last Ascent

After being stormbound on July 30, Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar, and Phinsoo climb toward Camp VII once more.

Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar, Phinsoo,
and Dudley Wolfe were never seen alive again.

August 2, 1939

Something Awful

Tsering Norbu waits two days at Camp VI, then runs down the mountain in a single morning.

Durrance writes in his diary: "The Sherpas are certain something awful has happened."

Wiessner refuses to believe it — it seems impossible to him that anything could have happened to so able a group.

August 3–7, 1939

The Last Search

With no one left fit to climb, Wiessner sets out anyway with Tsering Norbu and Dawa Thondup. It takes them all day to reach Camp I.

A storm pins them there until August 7. Then Tsering Norbu corrects his earlier account: they had found Wolfe at Camp VII with no food at all.

Even Wiessner loses hope. Wolfe and the three Sherpas are gone.

Chapter V · July 19, 1939

800 Feet from Glory

No one had ever stood on an 8,000-meter summit. By nightfall, two men were an easy snow plateau away from being first.

09:00 · Camp IX · 26,050 ft

Wiessner and Pasang Dawa Lama left camp with the summit 2,200 feet above them — a late start.

At a point of decision, Wiessner faced two ways up: a traverse right into the couloir later named the Bottleneck, beneath unstable ice from the summit cornice — or a direct rock climb to the left, free of falling ice but brutally difficult. He chose the rock.

Nightfall · ~27,450 ft

Nine hours of climbing followed, at a technical standard unprecedented at that altitude — a line that, as of Viesturs and Roberts' 2009 account, has never been repeated.

By dark they stood at roughly 27,450 feet — "about 27,500," by Wiessner's own report. Above them lay 800 feet of easy snow. Wiessner wanted to push on through the night and finish it.

Pasang Lama refused, and would not pay out the rope. Wiessner agreed to turn back — he did not go on alone, and through all the bitterness to come, he never once criticized his partner.

The Descent

Going back down the cliffs in darkness, Pasang Lama lost both pairs of crampons he was carrying.

"…many times during that descent I regretted intensely that I had not insisted on continuing over that last short traverse." — Fritz Wiessner, American Alpine Journal, 1940
July 21 · The Second Attempt

They tried again by the Bottleneck. Without crampons, in bad snow, it was hopeless. They returned to Camp IX. The summit was finished.

Jim Curran's later verdict: had Wiessner taken the couloir on the 19th, the pair might have summited and returned to camp the same day — the first humans atop an eight-thousander, without oxygen, fifteen years before Annapurna — and, with the high camps still stocked and manned as Wiessner believed they were, everyone might have come home.

Chapter VI

The Long Wait

While two men fought for the summit, the mountain beneath them was quietly emptying out. His dot still marks Camp VII.

While Wiessner and Pasang Lama fought for the summit, Dudley Wolfe waited at Camp VIII, where his body had given out at the bergschrund on July 17. He would come down only as far as Camp VII — and never a foot below it under his own power. And beneath them both, the expedition had been quietly coming apart for weeks. The team was splitting along class lines — Wolfe drawn closer to Wiessner, possibly because he was paying most of the bills. After the eight-day storm, only Wiessner and Wolfe still wanted the summit; everyone else below had lost the will. The support parties were astonished by Wiessner's progress but had barely been functioning, and at Camp II, Durrance found Cromwell and Trench in complete apathy.

What happened next was a cascade of misread signals — hurried instructions in a borrowed language, an order meant for the valley, three shouts into silence. Each step of it was reasonable. Each step of it was wrong.

The signals · From the expedition record

July 13 — Durrance, collapsing, leaves rushed instructions for the Sherpas to ferry supplies upward. The Sherpas and the Americans share no common language; the instructions may never have been understood.

July 18 — Cromwell orders the lower camps cleared. As far as the world below the Shoulder is concerned, the expedition is going home.

July 20 — Tse Tendrup climbs to within 500 feet of Camp VIII and shouts, three times, into the silence. No answer comes. There is fresh avalanche debris. He concludes the summit party is dead.

July 20–21 — Believing the men above are gone, and seeing the lower camps already coming down, the Sherpas strike Camps VI and VII — tents, mattresses, sleeping bags. By July 23 they are at Base Camp.

In Wiessner's account, Tendrup, "calling up to Camp VIII but receiving no answer, convinced the others that we three — the summit party — had been killed high up on the mountain and that they… should salvage what they could — i.e., the sleeping-bags and mattresses."

By the time Wiessner and Pasang Lama came back down to him on July 22, Wolfe had been alone for days — out of matches, unable to cook or even melt ice for water. And at Camp VII that evening, a second shock: the tents collapsed under snow, no mattresses, one sleeping bag among three men, food scattered. The camp had been stripped.

"Utterly unanticipated, we found Camp VII had been largely evacuated… We could not conceive of what had happened to create such a situation… all sorts of fantastic thoughts crossed our minds." — Fritz Wiessner, American Alpine Journal, 1940

Wiessner left Wolfe at Camp VII — at Wolfe's own request, he later said — and descended with Pasang Lama, expecting to find supplies one camp down, still intending another summit attempt. Camp by camp they found nothing: little food, no sleeping bags, no people.

Wolfe was now the highest human being on Earth — and entirely alone.

No one below could picture what the wait was doing to him — the effects of living for weeks above 22,000 feet were simply not yet understood. When the rescuers finally reached him on July 29, they found what the mountain had done: Wolfe apathetic, trapped in a tent fouled with his own waste, uninterested in the letters they had carried up to him, unwilling to come down.

Chapter VII

Fifty Years of Silence

The blame shifted like snow — from Wiessner, to Durrance. For fifty years, one man said nothing.

The fight over blame began before the mourning did. At Base Camp on July 24, Wiessner — enraged at what he had found on the way down — accused first Cromwell and then Tendrup of trying to murder him; Cromwell, in turn, accused Wiessner of abandoning Wolfe. They were enemies for life within the hour.

The recriminations began at Bandipora on August 27, when Cromwell saw Wiessner's draft report and — by Durrance's diary, the only record of the scene — shouted that Wiessner had murdered Wolfe and the Sherpas. Cromwell and Trench had already cabled the AAC and spread their account through the British community at Srinagar. Back in America, Wiessner gave a disastrous interview to the New York Times — reported ever afterward, though the exact words were never verified, as saying that in the high mountains, as in war, one had to expect casualties. The climbing world took sides. An AAC inquiry produced a report so bland it settled nothing; the American consul's official report, too, was built on partial evidence — its author had seen Wiessner's account but never the letters of Cromwell or Trench. Both Wiessner and Cromwell resigned from the club. In Maine, Wolfe's brother Clifford considered a lawsuit, then let it drop.

How the news broke · The Boston Globe · August 24, 1939 · Page 13

Dudley Wolfe Is Killed Trying to Climb 'K2'

Boston Sportsman Died July 30 on Mt. Godwin Austen, India, With Guide

ROCKLAND, Me., Aug. 23 — Clifford Smith of Boston and Warrenton Park, Rockport, today received a cablegram announcing the death of his brother, Dudley Wolfe… Owing to the severity of the weather it was impossible to recover the bodies, and the expedition has just made its way back to civilization.

The family learned by private cable days before the wider press took up the story — and the dispatch, like many first reports, got details wrong: it gave Wolfe's age as 44. He was 43.

For seventeen years, Wiessner bore the public blame. Then in 1956 he published his account — and with it a single piece of evidence: the story of something he said he had found, years before, on the floor of a tent at Camp II. The blame swung to Durrance, and stayed there. Wiessner was rehabilitated, made an honorary member of the AAC in 1966, and by the 1980s was revered.

Durrance said nothing. For thirty-three more years.

Wiessner died in 1988. Only then, in 1989, did Durrance open his handwritten expedition diary — sealed for fifty years — to the researchers Kauffman and Putnam. What Wiessner said he had found, what the diary recorded, and what was never found at all: the two accounts are below. Weigh them yourself.

"One of the worst tragedies in the climbing history of the Himalaya." — Fosco Maraini, 1961
The Evidence

The Note That Was Never Found

Two accounts, published thirty-three years apart. Tap each document to weigh it.

Published 1956 · Seventeen years after

Wiessner’s account

Wiessner produced the story of a crumpled note, found on the floor of the tent at Camp II during his descent. It was, he said, in Durrance’s handwriting, and it announced the order to bring the sleeping bags down the mountain.

He said he had handed the note to the American Alpine Club’s committee of inquiry in 1939. Durrance never publicly refuted the story.

EXHIBIT: the note itself — never found, despite careful searches of the archives.
Opened 1989 · After Wiessner’s death

Durrance’s diary

For fifty years Durrance said nothing. Only after Wiessner died in 1988 did he open his handwritten expedition diary to the researchers Kauffman and Putnam.

It records that the order to clear the lower camps came from Cromwell, on July 18 — and that a note carried up to the Sherpas at Camp VII was most likely Cromwell’s, not his.

EXHIBIT: the diary — kept sealed for half a century.
Kauffman & Putnam’s conclusion: Durrance had kept his silence to protect two men — Wiessner and Cromwell — who were, by the time the diary opened, both dead. Wherever the note came from, it has never surfaced. Even now the verdict is not unanimous: Jordan and, later, Viesturs and Roberts point less to any single culprit than to the unplanned stripping of the camps itself, and Mick Conefrey’s The Ghosts of K2 steers a deliberate middle course. And yet, as Jim Curran wrote, it was so nearly an outstanding success — one route choice below the summit; one shout answered at Camp VIII; one order, better understood.

The expedition ended in August 1939. Its story did not. The survivors carried it — some in silence, some in exile — through the rest of long lives, and the mountain went on writing epilogues of its own.

Fritz Wiessner
1900 – 1988 · Stowe, Vermont
He sailed home into the outbreak of war — a German-American at, as Viesturs and Roberts put it, "perhaps the worst time in the twentieth century to be one" — and was even accused of spying. Robert Underhill's defense: "He had the guts — and there is no single thing finer in a climber, or in a man." Made an honorary member of the AAC in 1966, he led climbs until nearly 87. A quiet stand of woods above Stowe is his only permanent memorial.
Jack Durrance
1912 – 2003 · Denver, Colorado
In October 1941 he led the rescue of a parachutist stranded on top of Devils Tower — climbing in tennis shoes, the last 400 feet of the descent lit by car headlights — and then never climbed again. He became a pulmonary physician and a celebrated breeder of irises, one of them named "Gorby." Of 1939 he said: "I knew I was not responsible for those men dying. But I never felt I could argue against Wiessner."
Dawa Thondup
1907 – 1973
The Sherpa who turned back ill from the first rescue attempt climbed on for three more decades. John Hunt requested him by name for Everest in 1953 — at 48 the oldest member of the expedition, he carried to the South Col against a doctor's warning — and in 1955 he carried high on the first ascent of Kangchenjunga.
Alice Damrosch Kiaer
1892 – 1967
Wolfe's ex-wife — who had gone on living with him after the divorce — had organized and managed the first U.S. Women's Olympic Ski Team in 1936, and in 1938 helped the skiing pioneer Hannes Schneider and his family escape the Nazis. She was elected to the U.S. National Ski Hall of Fame in 1969.
Tony Cromwell
1892 – 1987
He resigned from the AAC in the inquiry's wake, keeping his many first ascents in the Canadian Rockies; Mount Cromwell, in Jasper National Park, was named for him in 1972. He died in February 1987, seventeen months before Wiessner. The two men never reconciled.
1953 · The Gilkey Memorial
K2's cenotaph
On Charlie Houston's 1953 expedition, the geologist Art Gilkey was struck down at 25,500 feet by thrombophlebitis — illness at altitude was still barely understood, and Houston himself would become a pioneering researcher of high-altitude medicine. During the desperate lowering, Pete Schoening's single ice-axe belay held six falling men; Gilkey was then swept away. The cairn his teammates raised became the mountain's memorial to all its dead — the place where Dudley Wolfe's name now rests.
July 31, 1954 · The First Ascent
Fifteen years to the day
K2 was finally climbed by Lino Lacedelli and Achille Compagnoni of Ardito Desio's Italian expedition — fifteen years to the day after Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar and Phinsoo started up toward Camp VII for the last time.
August 2008 · The Bottleneck
The couloir Wiessner refused
On July 19, 1939, Wiessner judged the ice couloir below the summit too dangerous and took the rock instead. On August 1–2, 2008, a serac collapsed into that couloir — by then named the Bottleneck — cutting the fixed lines; eleven climbers died, the worst single accident in K2's history. As the American Alpine Club's reviewer put it: he was right.

In Memoriam

Those who did not return

1919 · Passport photo
Dudley Francis Cecil Wolfe
February 6, 1896 — c. July 30, 1939 · Camp VII, 24,700 ft
Socialite, decorated ambulance driver, transatlantic racing captain, Harvard graduate, heir. Gentle, shy, stubborn. He spent 38 continuous days above 22,000 feet without oxygen — an endurance no one had then matched even with it.
1939 · Durrance Coll. · AAC
Pasang Kikuli
1911 — c. July 31, 1939 · Sirdar · Sola Khumbu
The most experienced high-altitude climber in the world. A survivor of the 1934 Nanga Parbat catastrophe, decorated with the German Red Cross Medal for staying beside a dying man. Climbed 7,000 feet in a single day to reach the man everyone else had given up on — then went up one final time on July 31. He left a widow and two young children. Wiessner: "The death of Pasang Kikuli takes away one of the finest men and best climbers from the climbing fraternity."
PK
Pasang Kitar
Sherpa · Rescue Party
Went high on the first rescue attempt, reached Wolfe on the second, and volunteered again for the last. Last seen climbing toward Camp VII on July 31, 1939.
P
Phinsoo
Sherpa · K2 1938 · Rescue Party
Veteran of the 1938 reconnaissance. Reached Wolfe at Camp VII on July 29 and climbed toward him again two days later. Disappeared with Pasang Kikuli, Pasang Kitar, and Wolfe.
Pasang Dawa Lama
The one who turned them back · First ascent of Cho Oyu, October 19, 1954
The Sherpa who refused the rope at 27,500 feet lived to make history. On October 19, 1954, with Herbert Tichy and Sepp Jöchler, he made the first ascent of Cho Oyu — 8,188 metres — and climbed it again in 1958. Everest and K2 had both been taken on bottled oxygen; Nanga Parbat, climbed without it the year before, stood 62 metres lower. Cho Oyu was the highest summit anyone had yet reached breathing nothing but the mountain's own air, and it would hold that record until Messner and Habeler's Everest in 1978. "Pasang embraced me," Tichy wrote of the summit. "The tears that ran down his cheeks were blown away into eternity as crystals of ice."

The mountain kept them for half a century, and then began to give them back. In 1993 the glacier returned Pasang Kikuli; in 1995, remains believed to be his companions'. And on July 12, 2002, the writer Jennifer Jordan and the filmmaker Jeff Rhoads, walking the Godwin-Austen below the south face, came upon a debris field "full of leather and hemp rope and bits of canvas… stuff that hadn't been used in decades" — and then the glacier gave up Dudley Wolfe himself: skeletal remains, vintage gear, and a leather mitten marked "Wolfe." In sixty-three years his bones and gear had traveled a mile and a half down the glacier — about four inches a day. What could be gathered was laid to rest at the Gilkey Memorial, his name inscribed on a dinner plate found among the debris.

"They have, as their monument, a more beautiful structure than any man will ever erect, K2." — Fritz Wiessner, American Alpine Journal, 1940
