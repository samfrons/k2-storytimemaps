One Day

Six climbers summited K2 on 13 August 1995, and the storm killed them all coming down. The papers put only one of them on trial.

"It is better to live one day as a tiger than a thousand years as a sheep." — a line Alison Hargreaves gave an interviewer, and came to regret handing the world

She had already done the impossible thing. On May 13, 1995, Alison Hargreaves stood on the summit of Everest without bottled oxygen and without Sherpa support — the first woman ever to climb it that way. Three months later she stood on the summit of K2, the harder and crueller mountain, in the last good light of a fine evening.

She was dead within hours. And then, because she was a mother of two, the mountain's verdict was overruled by a louder one — delivered not by the weather, but by the front pages of her own country.

Chapter I

The Tiger

Establish her before the press gets its hands on her.

Alison Hargreaves was thirty-three, from Derbyshire, and one of the finest mountaineers Britain had produced. In 1993 she had set out to solo the six great north faces of the Alps in a single season. She had climbed the north face of the Eiger while six months pregnant — a fact the papers would later hold against her as though the child had objected.

Her ambition in 1995 was singular and clean: the three highest mountains on Earth — Everest, K2, Kangchenjunga — without supplemental oxygen. In May she took the first. On June 11 she flew to Pakistan for the second.

She had two young children at home, Tom and Kate. She missed them with an intensity she never hid. She also could not stop climbing, and did not believe she should have to choose — a position that costs a man nothing and, in the summer of 1995, would cost her everything.

Chapter II · June – July

The Waiting Game

The weather that summer was foul and relentless — and the forecast panels kept coming back wrong.

She reached Base Camp and joined the international crowd at the foot of the mountain — Spanish climbers of Montañeros de Aragón of Zaragoza and the Peña Guara club of Huesca, an American team, New Zealanders, Canadians. Through June and July she made attempt after attempt, and the mountain turned her back every time.

By early August the season was closing. The Americans called for porters and began to leave — all but Rob Slater, who stayed to try once more with the New Zealanders. Slater had never climbed above 7,000 metres; K2 would be his first seven-thousander and his first eight-thousander at once. He was not going home.

Hargreaves planned one more attempt, in August. She was warned not to — in those conditions, on that mountain, others told her, it could kill her.

The Wire · June – August 1995

The season, as it moved

What follows is the 1995 season over the real terrain of K2 — every dated position drawn from the record. Each figure is a rope team. Watch how close together the prudent and the dead stood.

June – July

The mountain refuses the season

Storm after storm. Hargreaves makes repeated attempts through June and July; every team on the mountain is turned back. Earlier in the season, Jordi Anglès dies in a fall on K2 — the summer's first loss.

August 12–13

The last window — Camp 4

Four clear days. The remnants of the American team and Hargreaves join the New Zealand and Canadian climbers at Camp 4, ≈7,600 m — at least twelve hours below the summit.

August 13 · Midday

Hillary reads the sky

Above Camp 4, Peter Hillary — son of Everest's pioneer — judges that the weather which has held for four days is beginning to change. He turns around. Jeff Lakes also turns back, below the summit.

August 13 · 6:45 PM

Six on the summit

In fine, windless conditions: Hargreaves and Javier Olivar, then Rob Slater, Javier Escartín, Lorenzo Ortíz, Bruce Grant. Hours too late to be there — but the sky is clear and the air is still.

August 13 · Night

The storm on the descent

Winds estimated at more than 160 kilometres per hour tear across the summit slopes in the dark. All six who stood on the summit die coming down. Not one of them reaches a tent.

August 14

Two men descend through the wreckage

Pepe Garcés and Lorenzo Ortas, who rode out the storm at Camp 4, start down — frostbitten, exhausted. Before Camp 3 they find a bloodstained anorak, a single boot, a harness. They recognise the gear.

August 15

The mountain takes the prudent, too

Jeff Lakes, who turned back below the summit — the correct choice, the Hillary choice — fights his way down to a lower camp, and dies there of exposure.

Late August

Never brought down

Garcés and Ortas survive six days without a tent and are airlifted out. No body from the summit party is recovered. K2 keeps her, the way it keeps most of the people it takes.

Chapter III · August 13 · 6:45 PM

The Summit

The single most beautiful beat of the season. The disaster is already inside this moment, invisible.

P401K2 FORECAST13 AUG 1995
KARAKORAM  8611M   OUTLOOK: FINE
FOURTH CLEAR DAY   WIND: LIGHT
SUMMIT WINDOW ............ OPEN
LATE CHANGE POSSIBLE .... WATCH
▮

At 6:45 in the evening, in fine conditions, Alison Hargreaves and the Spaniard Javier Olivar reached the summit of K2. Behind them came Rob Slater — the first eight-thousander of his life, and he had made it. Then Javier Escartín and Lorenzo Ortíz. Then the New Zealander Bruce Grant. Six people on the second-highest point on Earth, in the last good light of the day.

It was hours too late to be there. The safe turnaround on K2 falls in the early afternoon. But the sky was clear and the air was still, and standing on that summit it would have been almost impossible to believe the mountain meant to kill them all.

It did.

Lower on the mountain, Peter Hillary was already descending. He had read the same sky and turned back — the decision that saved his life, and the decision the papers would never think to praise, because he did not die and so had no lesson to be made of him.

Chapter IV · That Night

The Storm
on the Descent

The forecast panel keeps scrolling — a machine reporting a catastrophe it cannot feel.

TEMP FALLING FAST   VIS: NIL
SIX CLIMBERS ABOVE 8000M

The storm came in with a violence the survivors below could barely comprehend. On nearby Broad Peak, Scott Fischer described brutal cold and winds past 160 kilometres an hour. On K2's summit slopes, in the dark, all six who had stood on top died coming down. Not one of them reached a tent.

The Canadian Jeff Lakes had turned back below the summit earlier in the day — the correct choice. He fought his way down through the storm and reached one of the lower camps alive. The mountain took him anyway: he died of exposure on August 15.

Seven dead. And the papers, six thousand kilometres away, had already chosen which one of them they would put on trial.

Chapter V · August 14

The Anorak
in the Snow

The most restrained beat of the story, before the fury of the next one.

Two Spanish climbers, Pepe Garcés and Lorenzo Ortas, had survived the storm at Camp 4. The next day they began to descend, frostbitten and half-destroyed — they would end up airlifted out after six days without a tent.

On the way down, before Camp 3, they came upon a bloodstained anorak in the snow. A single climbing boot. A harness. They recognised the gear. It was Alison's.

From Camp 3 they could see a body in the distance, lower on the mountain. They did not go to it — they had nothing left, and could barely save themselves. So it was never positively identified. But they had little doubt. The wind, they concluded, had blown her from the mountain.

She was never brought down. K2 kept her, the way it keeps most of the people it takes.

The Trial

The Headline vs. The Human

The facts were never in dispute. Only the judgement was — and the judgement was a lie the size of a front page. Tap each headline to peel it back.

The front pages below are period-style pastiches — invented mastheads, no real newspaper reproduced. The judgements they carry were made, in those words' spirit, across the British press in August 1995.

Rob Slater died on the same descent, in the same storm, chasing the first eight-thousander of his life. Bruce Grant died. Javier Olivar, Javier Escartín, Lorenzo Ortíz, Jeff Lakes — all died. Not one of their obituaries led with whether they were fathers. Five of the seven dead were men, and the mountain had not asked any of them for a note from home.

DAILY CLARION
MOTHER OF TWO DIES ON KILLER PEAK

No newspaper asked how Rob Slater could leave the people who loved him, or interrogated the parenting of the fathers on that mountain. The question was reserved, exclusively, for the woman — as though ambition in a mother were a crime, and as though the risk she took were somehow more selfish than the identical risk taken by the men who died beside her.

THE SUNDAY MERIDIAN
HOW COULD SHE LEAVE HER CHILDREN?
Tap to peel back the page ⌄

She was one of the most technically accomplished climbers of her generation, who had soloed Everest without oxygen three months earlier. She was warned about the August window and weighed it as a professional. So did Peter Hillary, who read the same sky, turned back, and lived. The difference between "reckless" and "prudent" was, that day, a matter of hours and luck. The difference in how the press treated the dead was a matter of sex.

EVENING SENTINEL
SELFISH? RECKLESS? THE WOMAN WHO GAMBLED WITH DEATH
Tap to peel back the page ⌄

She said it once, to an interviewer — better to live one day as a tiger than a thousand years as a sheep — and the papers handed it back to her as an epitaph and an indictment in the same breath. It was quoted as proof of a death wish. It was never read as what it plainly was: the ordinary creed of every serious mountaineer, man or woman, alive or dead, who has ever chosen the mountain knowing what the mountain can do.

THE NATIONAL EXAMINER
"ONE DAY AS A TIGER" — HER OWN WORDS CONDEMN HER

The verdict this page returns: the mountain killed six climbers on 13 August 1995, without preference or malice. It did not know which of them was a mother. Everything cruel said afterward was said by people who were warm, and safe, and reading a newspaper.
Chapter VI · Epilogue

The Ones
Who Came After

The newsprint drops away here. The press is gone. Only the family is left.

Her children grew up. Her son, Tom Ballard, became a climber of extraordinary gift. He spoke of his mother without bitterness, and he climbed toward her.

In February 2019, Tom Ballard died on Nanga Parbat — in the same Pakistani ranges that had taken Alison. Mother and son, both of them, lost to the great peaks.

The tigers, it turned out, ran in the family. So did the mountains' habit of keeping what they love.
