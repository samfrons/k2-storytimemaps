1986 is when K2's arithmetic changed. Everything the modern mountain would become — the crowds, the brilliance, the new lines, the storm that shuts the door at 8,000 metres — happened in one summer, on one slide carousel. Twenty-seven up · thirteen gone
Chapter I

Nine Expeditions

The strongest alpinists of a golden generation converged on one mountain at once — Poles, Italians, French, Austrians, British, Americans, Koreans.

By 1986, K2 had been climbed by exactly one line: the Abruzzi Spur, the ridge of the 1939 tragedy, finally completed by the Italians in 1954. That summer, nine expeditions gathered below the mountain to change the map — the cream of the golden age of Himalayan climbing, all at once.

There were the Poles of the high-altitude school that was rewriting the sport — Jerzy Kukuczka and Tadeusz Piotrowski aiming straight up the unclimbed south face, Wanda Rutkiewicz aiming to be the first woman on the summit. The Italian Renato Casarotto came to solo the south-southwest pillar — the "Magic Line." The French husband and wife Maurice and Liliane Barrard climbed as a pair, high-altitude romantics. The Austrians and the British international team took the Abruzzi; with the British came Alan Rouse, and the film team of Kurt Diemberger and Julie Tullis, who had been working the great peaks together since 1981.

Between June 21 and August 10, twenty-seven climbers reached the summit — more than in the previous thirty-two years combined. Thirteen people did not come home. The mountain spent the whole summer teaching the same lesson, and nobody could afford to hear it until it was over.

The Ledger · June 21 – August 11, 1986

One summer, frame by frame

What follows is the Black Summer over the real terrain of K2 — every dated position drawn from the 1986 record, across three faces of the mountain at once. Each figure is a rope team.

Positions approximate along the routes; dates and altitudes as documented in 1986.

June 21

An avalanche below the Negrotto Col

The Americans John Smolich and Alan Pennington are swept away by an avalanche low on the south-southwest route. Pennington's body is recovered and buried near Base Camp. The summer is one day old.

June 23

The first woman on K2

Wanda Rutkiewicz summits by the Abruzzi — the first woman ever to stand on K2. Thirty minutes behind her come Liliane and Maurice Barrard, with Michel Parmentier. Liliane is the second woman on top, beside her husband.

June 24

The Barrards do not come down

Descending after a high bivouac, the Barrards fall behind and vanish on the upper mountain. Rutkiewicz and Parmentier make it down. Liliane's body is found three weeks later at the mountain's foot.

July 8

The Polish Line

Kukuczka and Piotrowski summit K2 by a new route straight up the south face, in alpine style — a line of such danger it has never been repeated in full. They top out late, into worsening weather.

July 10

Piotrowski's crampons

Descending the Abruzzi, exhausted, Tadeusz Piotrowski loses first one crampon, then the other, and falls past Kukuczka — two days after the greatest climb of his life.

July 16

Casarotto, within sight of the tents

Retreating from his third solo attempt on the Magic Line, Renato Casarotto breaks through an ice bridge minutes from safety and falls 40 metres into a crevasse. He is brought out alive, and dies at its lip.

August 3–4

The Magic Line falls — at a price

Piasecki, Božík and Wróż complete Casarotto's pillar and descend by the Abruzzi in the dark. Wojciech Wróż slips from the end of a fixed rope and is lost. Low on the Spur, the porter Muhammad Ali is killed by stonefall.

August 4

The first Briton · the film team at 7 PM

Alan Rouse summits around 4 PM — the first Briton on K2. Mrufka Wolf turns back below the top. Around 7 PM, Kurt Diemberger and Julie Tullis — the highest film team in the world — stand on the summit they have chased for years.

August 4–5

Seven in the trap

Tullis falls on the descent; Diemberger's belay holds. They bivouac, then reach Camp IV on the Shoulder, ≈8,000 m — where a storm slams the door. Seven climbers are pinned in the tents in the death zone.

August 6–7

The night the storm took Julie

Battered by the fall and the altitude, Julie Tullis dies in the night at Camp IV. The storm does not pause. The six left have almost no food, and the gas for melting snow is running out.

August 10

The break for life

The snowfall stops; five stagger out. Rouse cannot stand and is left in his tent. Imitzer and Wieser collapse a few hundred feet from camp. Bauer, Diemberger and Mrufka fight down toward the ropes.

August 10–11

Two men come down

Willi Bauer, then Kurt Diemberger, reach Base Camp — frostbitten, barely alive. Mrufka Wolf never arrives: a year later a Japanese team finds her still clipped to the fixed ropes. The Black Summer's toll stands at thirteen.

Chapter II · June 23

The First Woman

Wanda Rutkiewicz reached the top ahead of the couple climbing behind her — and only one of the three women on K2 that summer came home.

On June 23, 1986, Wanda Rutkiewicz of Poland became the first woman to stand on the summit of K2 — climbing without supplemental oxygen, at the front of the era's hardest school of mountaineering. Half an hour behind her came Liliane Barrard, beside her husband Maurice, with Michel Parmentier: the first married couple on the summit of the second-highest mountain on Earth.

The Barrards climbed the way they lived — light, high, together. On the descent, after a bivouac high on the mountain, they fell behind. Rutkiewicz and Parmentier came down; the Barrards never did. Three weeks later the mountain gave Liliane back, at its foot, and she was buried near Base Camp. Maurice it kept until 1998.

24 JUNE — SUMMIT PARTY BIVOUACKED HIGH. RUTKIEWICZ, PARMENTIER DESCENDING SLOW. BARRARDS NOT SEEN SINCE MORNING.

25 JUNE — WATCHING THE UPPER MOUNTAIN THROUGH THE LONG LENS. NOTHING MOVES ABOVE THE SHOULDER.

It was the summer's pattern in miniature, set in its first week: a magnificent first, and a descent that cost more than the summit was worth. The season's ledger would repeat it, line by line.

Chapter III · July 8

The Polish Line

Straight up the unclimbed south face, in alpine style — a route so dangerous it has never been repeated in full.

Jerzy Kukuczka was in the middle of the greatest run in the history of high-altitude climbing — racing Reinhold Messner to all fourteen 8,000-metre peaks, and doing it by new routes and in winter. For K2 he chose the unclimbed south face, with Tadeusz Piotrowski: no fixed camps above the face's base, no oxygen, no retreat once committed.

On July 8, in deteriorating weather, the two Poles pulled over the top of the face and stood on the summit. The route — the "Polish Line" — was hailed at once as one of the boldest climbs ever made at that altitude, and it has never been repeated in full.

The mountain collected its fee on the way down the Abruzzi. On July 10, exhausted, out of food and descending in bad snow, Piotrowski stopped to wait for Kukuczka. Then, downclimbing steep ice: one crampon came off. As he reached to fix it — the other. Tadeusz Piotrowski fell past his partner and was gone. Kukuczka descended alone, and lived to finish the fourteen.

Chapter IV · July 16

The Soloist

Renato Casarotto survived the Magic Line three times. The glacier under his own tent-line did not let him pass.

Renato Casarotto was the finest solo climber of his generation, and he had come to K2 for the hardest unclimbed line on the mountain: the south-southwest pillar, the Magic Line — alone. Three times he went up. Three times the mountain turned him back, the last from nearly 8,300 metres.

On July 16 he gave it up and came down for good. A short distance from Base Camp — on flat glacier, the climb finished, his wife Goretta waiting at the tents — an ice bridge collapsed under him, and he fell 40 metres into a crevasse.

He was still alive. He raised the alarm on his radio, and climbers ran onto the glacier and hauled him to the surface. He tried to take a few steps, and died at the lip of the crevasse — of internal injuries the fall had already decided. He was laid to rest, at Goretta's wish, in the crevasse that had taken him.

Chapter V · August 3–4

The Magic Line

The pillar Casarotto died leaving was climbed eighteen days later — and it, too, took a life on the way down.

On August 3, the Poles Przemysław Piasecki and Wojciech Wróż, with the Slovak Peter Božík, completed the Magic Line — the third new route of the summer, and to this day one of the hardest ever climbed on K2.

They descended the Abruzzi in the dark, exhausted, rappelling and downclimbing a web of old fixed ropes. Somewhere above Camp IV, Wojciech Wróż slipped from the end of a fixed rope and fell to his death — the summer's cruellest arithmetic: a first ascent for a life, hours apart.

The same days took the season's quietest casualty: Muhammad Ali, a Pakistani sirdar working for the Korean expedition, killed by stonefall low on the Spur — the man on the mountain who had not come to climb it.

Chapter VI · August 4–10

Seven in the Trap

The summit day was jubilant: the first Briton, the film team at seven in the evening. Then the storm shut the door at 8,000 metres.

On August 4 the Abruzzi gave up its last summits of the summer. Around four in the afternoon, Alan Rouse broke trail to the top and became the first Briton to climb K2; Mrufka Wolf, climbing with him, turned back below the summit. Around seven in the evening came Kurt Diemberger and Julie Tullis — the "highest film team in the world," partners on the great peaks since 1981, standing at last on the mountain they had circled for years.

It was far too late in the day, and the price began immediately. On the descent Tullis slipped and pulled Diemberger off; his belay held both, but the fall left her hurt. They bivouacked in the open above 8,300 metres, then crawled into Camp IV at about 8,000 metres — as a storm arrived and sealed the Shoulder shut.

Seven climbers were now pinned in the death zone: Rouse and Mrufka, Diemberger and Tullis, and the Austrians Willi Bauer, Alfred Imitzer and Hannes Wieser — with a handful of food, failing gas, and tents that the wind was tearing apart.

Julie Tullis — the first British woman to summit K2 — died in the night of August 6–7, in the tent, beside the partner she had filmed the mountains with for fifteen years. She was buried on the mountainside. The storm kept blowing for three more days.

Chapter VII · August 10

The Break for Life

When the snow finally stopped, five went for the door. Two came through it.

On the morning of August 10, after five days of storm, the snowfall stopped. Alan Rouse — the strongest of them a week before, who had broken trail to the summit — could no longer stand. He was left in his tent, delirious; there was no way on earth to carry him. Alfred Imitzer and Hannes Wieser collapsed within a few hundred feet of the camp and could not be raised.

Three kept moving: Willi Bauer in front, breaking the trail; Mrufka Wolf; Kurt Diemberger behind. Somewhere on the endless fixed ropes below the Shoulder, in the wind and the coming dark, Mrufka fell out of the world's sight. A year later, a Japanese expedition found her — still attached to the fixed ropes, upright, as though the mountain had simply stopped her clock.

Bauer staggered into Base Camp on the evening of the 10th, past frostbite, barely able to speak; Diemberger came down behind him. Of the seven who had been trapped at Camp IV, two came home. The Black Summer was over. Thirteen were dead — and the arguments about what it all meant, in the books and journals of the decade that followed, never really ended.

Of the women of 1986: Wanda Rutkiewicz, the first woman on K2, climbed on — she died on Kangchenjunga in 1992, chasing the third of the three highest mountains, the same trilogy Alison Hargreaves would chase in 1995. The mountain's ledgers rhyme across the decades. That is why these pages exist together.
